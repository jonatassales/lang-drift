# Does a developer who only supervises a coding agent lose skill, and which Safe Seat decisions does the evidence support?

**Date:** 2026-11-03. **Question:** when a coding agent writes most of a ticket, what happens to the developer's understanding, skill and well-being, and which of the Safe Seat decisions the owner settled on 2026-11-03 does published evidence support, and which rest on product judgment alone? **Method:** every source was read in its primary form on 2026-11-03: the paper itself (arXiv HTML, an author-hosted PDF, or a full-text copy of the published article), the publisher's own blog post, or the FAA PDF. Every number below comes from that text; nothing was taken from news coverage. Three sources outside the starting list were added: Casner et al. (2014), which SAFO 17007 lists as related guidance, and two 2026 surveys found while checking the mental-health claim. Two searches looked for (a) a causal study linking agent-assisted work to depression or anxiety in developers and (b) a study that uses a share of hand-written code as an intervention; neither found one. Quotes are kept to a few words; the rest is paraphrase. Full citations are in section 10.

## 1. The question and why it matters for Safe Seat

Safe Seat keeps a developer writing a reserved part of every ticket by hand while a coding agent writes the rest. The design rests on four claims: a person who only supervises automation loses the skill needed to take over; writing a real problem by hand keeps understanding that reviewing does not; self-report cannot measure who wrote what; and a floor with a gate is a defensible way to hold the practice in place. This note checks each claim against the evidence, records each source's limits, and names the decisions that have no direct evidence, so the spec can cite the note instead of restating the studies.

## 2. Skill decay under automation

### 2.1 Bainbridge (1983), Ironies of Automation

**Found.** Bainbridge argues that automation can expand, not remove, the operator's problems. Physical skills deteriorate when unused, so an experienced operator who has only monitored may take over like an inexperienced one; and take-over happens when something is already wrong, so it needs more skill, not less ([p. 775](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf)). Retrieval of process knowledge depends on how often it is used, and that knowledge develops only through use and feedback on its effect; she adds that today's automated systems are monitored by former manual operators whose skills later generations cannot be expected to have ([pp. 775–776](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf)). Vigilance research shows people cannot sustain attention on a source where little happens for more than about half an hour, and an operator cannot check in real time a computer that decides faster than they can ([p. 776](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf)). Her proposed remedy is hands-on control for a short period in each shift, or simulator practice when that is impractical ([§2.3, p. 777](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf)). Following machine advice gives the operator no practice in reasoning, and automation that removes the easy parts of a task makes the hard parts harder; the most successful systems, which rarely need manual intervention, may need the greatest investment in training ([§2.3, §3, §3.1, p. 777](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf)). She also notes that skill is part of a worker's status, and a job reduced to monitoring is hard to accept ([§1.2, p. 776](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf)).

**Limits.** A five-page argument, not an experiment. It draws on 1970s process-control and flight-deck studies and contains no new data. It is not about software. Its remedies are proposals, untested in the paper. The text was read from a full-text mirror; the bibliographic record was checked against [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/0005-1098%2883%2990046-8).

### 2.2 Casner, Geven, Recker and Schooler (2014), manual flying skills in the automated cockpit

**Found.** Sixteen active Boeing 747-400 airline pilots flew routine and non-routine scenarios in a NASA simulator with automation switched on and off ([Method](https://gwern.net/doc/technology/2014-casner.pdf)). Hand-eye skills, instrument scanning and manual control, were mostly intact even when pilots reported rarely practising them. Cognitive skills were not: flying without the flight management computer, 7 of 16 pilots missed the missed-approach point by more than 3 nautical miles and 6 of 16 flew the wrong missed-approach heading; 75% followed a faulty altimeter until they were more than 300 feet off; 15 of 16 followed a failed airspeed indication until the stall warning ([Tables 5 and 7](https://gwern.net/doc/technology/2014-casner.pdf)). Pilots reported task-unrelated thought 20.0% of the time under full automation against 6.9% when flying by hand on raw data; navigation errors correlated with task-unrelated thought (r = .56, about 30% of the variance) and so did the number of stall warnings (r = .76) ([Results](https://gwern.net/doc/technology/2014-casner.pdf)). The authors conclude that retaining cognitive skills may depend on staying actively engaged while automation works, and recommend more frequent practice of those cognitive skills ([Summary and Recommendations](https://gwern.net/doc/technology/2014-casner.pdf)).

**Limits.** Sixteen pilots, one simulator session. Practice history was self-reported and not assigned, so the links between disuse, mind-wandering and errors are correlational. Aviation, not software. Peer-reviewed in *Human Factors*; read from a full-text copy of the published article.

## 3. Skill formation with AI assistance

### 3.1 Shen and Tamkin (2026), How AI Impacts Skill Formation

**Found.** A pre-registered randomized experiment: 52 participants, 26 per arm, each learning Trio, a Python async library none had used, through two tasks in at most 35 minutes ([§4.1, §4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)). The treatment arm had a GPT-4o chat assistant that could see their code and produce a full correct solution; afterwards both arms took a quiz without AI of 14 questions worth 27 points, covering debugging, code reading and concepts, with code writing excluded ([§4.1–4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)).

- **Skill.** The AI arm scored 4.15 points lower (Cohen's d = 0.738, p = 0.01), and the effect held after controlling for warm-up time (d = 0.725, p = 0.016) ([§5.2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)). The authors describe the gap as 17%, or two grade points. By simple arithmetic 4.15 of 27 is 15.4% of the maximum score; the companion post's text gives 50% against 67%, while its figure shows about 50% against about 65% ([Anthropic post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)). The direction and significance are clear; the exact percentage is not consistent across the authors' own statements.
- **Speed.** No significant speedup ([§5.2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)); the post's figure shows about 23 minutes with AI against about 25 without, p = 0.391 ([Anthropic post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)).
- **Which skill.** The largest gap was on debugging questions and the smallest on code reading. This breakdown was exploratory and not pre-registered ([§5.2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)).
- **Errors.** The median AI participant met 1 error, the median control participant 3, and the control arm met more errors specific to Trio's concepts; the authors attribute the control arm's learning to meeting and resolving errors on their own ([§6.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)).
- **Typing is not the mechanism.** Participants who retyped AI code by hand scored no better than those who pasted it; the authors conclude that cognitive effort matters more than time spent typing ([§6.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)).
- **Six interaction patterns** (cluster size, mean quiz score and time from the post's figure). Low-scoring: AI Delegation (n = 4, 39%, fastest at 19.5 min), Progressive AI Reliance (n = 4, 35%), Iterative AI Debugging (n = 4, 24%, slowest at 31 min). High-scoring: Generation-Then-Comprehension (n = 2, 86%), Hybrid Code-Explanation (n = 3, 68%), Conceptual Inquiry (n = 7, 65%, 22 min) ([§6](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245); [Anthropic post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)). High scorers asked for explanations or asked only conceptual questions.
- **Agents.** The authors expect agentic tools to remove even more human participation than a chat assistant, and call their chat setting a lower bound for cognitive offloading; this is a stated expectation, not a measurement ([§7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)).

**Limits.** One library, one session of about an hour, and a quiz minutes after the task: short-term comprehension, not retention ([§7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)). Crowd workers paid a flat 150 USD, with no job incentive to learn ([§4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)). A chat assistant, not an agent. The six patterns come from clusters of 2 to 7 people and are descriptive, not causal. On population, the post calls the participants "mostly junior", but the paper's balance table shows 29 of 52 with seven or more years of coding and 4 with one to three ([Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245)). A preprint (arXiv v2, 2026-02-01) by an Anthropic Fellow and an Anthropic researcher, published with a post by Anthropic, a developer of coding assistants.

### 3.2 Anthropic's post on the same study (2026-01-29)

**Found.** The [post](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills) adds no data beyond the paper. It draws two conclusions relevant here: managers should design how AI is deployed so engineers keep learning and can still oversee what they build, and cognitive effort, including getting stuck, is likely important for mastery. It points to learning modes in assistants as one design response.

**Limits.** The authors call the results preliminary, note the small sample, and say the quiz measured comprehension shortly after the task, so it does not show whether immediate scores predict long-term skill ([post, Conclusion](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)).

## 4. Productivity and self-perception

### 4.1 METR (2025), early-2025 AI and experienced open-source developers

**Found.** A randomized trial at the level of the issue: 16 experienced open-source developers completed 246 real issues (136 with AI allowed, 110 without), about two hours each, in mature repositories averaging 23,000 stars and 1.1 million lines of code, where each developer had about five years of history ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089); [§2.1, §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09089)). With AI allowed, mostly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet between February and June 2025, issues took 19% longer ([blog](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)); METR later gave the interval as +2% to +39% ([2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). Before starting, developers forecast a 24% speedup; after finishing, they still estimated 20%. Economics experts forecast 39% and machine-learning experts 38% ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)). Labeled screen recordings show that with AI developers spent a smaller share of time actively coding and searching, and more prompting, waiting on and reviewing output, and idle ([§3.2, Figure 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09089)).

**Limits.** Sixteen developers, each expert in a large codebase they know well; early-2025 tools. Time was self-reported, with screen recordings for a subset. METR states the result does not show that AI slows most developers, nor that later tools would ([blog FAQ](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)). It measures time, not skill.

### 4.2 METR (2026-02-24), change of study design

**Found.** A second study from August 2025: 57 developers (10 returning, 47 new) across 143 repositories and more than 800 tasks, median 10 years of experience, paid 50 USD an hour instead of 150 ([update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). Raw estimates: returning developers −18% time with AI (interval −38% to +9%), new developers −4% (−15% to +9%); both intervals include zero. METR judges the signal unreliable. More developers declined to take part because they did not want to work without AI; 30% to 50% of surveyed developers said they had not submitted some tasks because they did not want to do them without AI; one developer completed none of the tasks assigned to the no-AI arm; and time measurement is unreliable for developers running several agents at once, some of whom worked on unrelated tasks while an agent ran ([update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)).

**Limits.** A blog post, not a paper. METR calls its own estimate a likely lower bound and very weak evidence for the size of any change. The 30–50% figure is a survey of participants, not a measurement of dependence.

## 5. Critical thinking and cognitive engagement

### 5.1 Lee et al. (CHI 2025), generative AI and critical thinking

**Found.** A survey of 319 knowledge workers recruited on Prolific who use generative AI at work at least weekly (333 responses, 14 excluded), reporting 936 real examples of use ([§3.2–3.3](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)). Participants said they enacted critical thinking in 555 examples (59%) ([§3.3.1](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)). Higher confidence in the AI's ability to do the task went with less enacted critical thinking (β = −0.69, p < 0.001); higher confidence in doing the task oneself (β = 0.26, p = 0.026) and in evaluating AI output (β = 0.31, p = 0.046) went with more ([§4.2, Table 4](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)). For each of six cognitive activities, between 55% and 79% of examples were reported as needing less effort with AI ([§5.1](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)). Effort shifted from gathering information to verifying it, from solving problems to integrating AI output, and from doing the task to stewarding it ([§5.2](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)). Inhibitors included lack of time and seeing verification as someone else's job; the authors warn, citing Bainbridge, that abilities exercised only in rare high-stakes moments can deteriorate without routine practice ([§4.3.2](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)). Their design suggestions include explicit controls over how much the AI assists and framing critical engagement as skill development ([§6.1](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)).

**Limits.** Cross-sectional self-report; the authors state their analysis does not establish causation ([§6.1.1](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)). Participants sometimes conflated less effort using AI with less effort thinking critically; self-confidence is subjective; the sample skews young and technically skilled, English-speaking only, and mostly ChatGPT users ([§6.3, Table 3](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)). Not specific to software: computer and mathematical occupations were 59 of 319.

### 5.2 Kosmyna et al. (2025), Your Brain on ChatGPT

**Found.** Fifty-four participants wrote essays in three sessions in one of three conditions: an LLM, a search engine, or no tool; 18 of them returned for a fourth session with conditions swapped ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)). EEG showed the strongest, most distributed connectivity without tools, intermediate with search, and the weakest with the LLM. Participants moved from the LLM to no tool showed weaker alpha and beta connectivity. The LLM group reported the lowest ownership of their essays and struggled to quote what they had just written. The title frames the result as accumulated "cognitive debt"; the abstract reports that over four months the LLM group underperformed at neural, linguistic and behavioral levels ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)).

**Limits.** Essay writing, not code. Small: 18 in the crossover session. Participants came from a few academic institutions in one area; one model, ChatGPT ([project page](https://www.brainonllm.com/)). The authors say the work has not been peer-reviewed and its conclusions are preliminary ([project page](https://www.brainonllm.com/)). EEG connectivity is not a measure of skill. The 216-page PDF was too large for this note's tooling, so participant demographics were taken from the authors' summary, not the full text.

## 6. Burnout and well-being

### 6.1 Feng, Afroz and Sarma (2025), developer burnout and GenAI adoption

**Found.** A survey of 442 developers (688 responses before exclusions), recruited through 56 open-source communities and professional groups; 90% men, 360 with six or more years of industry experience ([§4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07435)). Using the Job Demands–Resources model and PLS-SEM, job demands tied to GenAI adoption (organizational pressure and workload) were associated with higher burnout (β = 0.398, p < .001), job resources (autonomy and learning resources) with lower burnout (β = −0.360), and favorable views of AI with lower burnout (β = −0.246); the model explains 39.8% of burnout's variance ([§5](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07435)). Autonomy was the stronger resource. Open responses describe adoption mandated so that a metric could be reported to management, learning shifting from mentoring and review to private copy-and-paste, and concern that juniors will take longer to build experience ([§5, §8.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07435)). The authors recommend KPIs that count verification costs and explicit time for juniors to learn ([§8.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07435)).

**Limits.** Cross-sectional. The abstract says adoption "heightens" burnout, but the authors' own limitations state the hypotheses are associations, not causal relationships, and name unmeasured confounders ([§7](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07435)). Burnout was four Likert items adapted from a prior study, not a clinical instrument ([§4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07435)). Self-selected, mostly male, open-source sample. arXiv preprint (v2, 2026-01-03); no venue stated.

### 6.2 Guizani et al. (2026), At What Cost?

**Found.** A position paper. It argues that GenAI can add cognitive load and unmeasured oversight labor, and raise expectations of output and pace, and it proposes a framework combining ambidexterity theory, ISO/IEC 25019 and the Job Demands–Resources model ([§3–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22349)). It notes that only a few studies measure burnout, stress or work–life balance in AI-assisted development, citing Feng et al. ([§1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22349)).

**Limits.** No new data. arXiv, submitted 2026-05-21 ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22349)).

### 6.3 Other evidence found while checking the mental-health claim

- **de Souza Santos et al. (2026)**, technostress among software professionals: an exploratory survey of 121 professionals in 26 countries with thematic analysis of open answers; the abstract names no clinical instrument and claims no causal effect ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15686)).
- **Perlis et al. (2026)**, *JAMA Network Open*: a cross-sectional, non-probability internet survey of 20,847 US adults, not developers, from 20 April to 27 May 2025. Daily generative-AI use was associated with moderate or greater depressive symptoms on the PHQ-9 (adjusted odds ratio 1.29, 95% CI 1.15–1.46), with similar patterns for anxiety on the GAD-2. The authors state the design cannot test causation and is equally consistent with depression leading to more AI use ([article](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.54820)).

## 7. Aviation practice

### 7.1 FAA SAFO 13002 (2013-01-04), Manual Flight Operations

**Found.** An FAA analysis of flight operations data, incidents and accidents found an increase in manual handling errors. Continuous use of autoflight does not reinforce manual skills and could degrade a pilot's ability to recover from an undesired state. Operators should give pilots appropriate opportunities to fly manually, for example in low-workload conditions, and make clear when automation should be used, for example under high workload ([SAFO 13002](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/other_visit/aviation_industry/airline_operators/airline_safety/SAFO13002.pdf)).

**Limits.** An advisory for operators, not a regulation. The underlying data analysis is cited, not published.

### 7.2 FAA SAFO 17007 (2017-05-04), Manual Flight Operations Proficiency

**Found.** Training should treat manual flight as the foundation of other flying skills and assess it. Line-operations policy should permit and encourage manual flight when conditions permit, leave the decision case by case to the pilot in command, and avoid two things: overly general rules, and quotas for manual flying "unless based on scientific evidence", with ten hours a month given as the example of what to avoid ([SAFO 17007, p. 3](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-11/SAFO17007.pdf)). The material comes from recommendations of the Flight Path Management Workgroup of the Air Carrier Training Aviation Rulemaking Committee, and the SAFO lists the NASA retention study (section 2.2) as related guidance ([pp. 1, 3](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-11/SAFO17007.pdf)).

**Limits.** Advisory, aviation. The PDF at the faa.gov address returned the document when requested with a browser user agent on 2026-11-03, so the quota point is cited to the FAA text itself; the Flight Safety Foundation article that first surfaced it is not needed as a source.

## 8. What the evidence does not show

- **No clinical effect is established.** Evidence that the shift to agent-assisted work causes depression or anxiety in developers is weak. The developer-specific sources are a cross-sectional burnout survey with non-clinical items (section 6.1), a position paper with no data (6.2) and an exploratory survey (6.3). The only study using clinical screens (PHQ-9, GAD-2) covers the general US population, is cross-sectional, and cannot separate cause from reverse cause (6.3). The search found no randomized or longitudinal study in developers. Safe Seat therefore makes no clinical claim and uses no clinical vocabulary.
- **No long-term skill outcome.** Shen and Tamkin measured comprehension minutes after a one-hour task; METR measured time, not skill; Casner measured pilots. No study found tracks a developer's skill over months of agent use.
- **No study of agents and learning.** The only randomized learning study used a chat assistant. The expectation that agents reduce learning further is the authors' stated hypothesis, not a result.
- **No study uses a share of hand-written code as an intervention.** No source read here, and nothing the search found, measures or manipulates a percentage of lines written by hand, or links a line share to skill. Shen and Tamkin's finding that retyping AI code taught no more than pasting it warns that lines typed are not the mechanism; engaged problem-solving is. The 5% floor is a product principle, not an evidence-derived threshold, and SAFO 17007 explicitly cautions against practice quotas without scientific basis.
- **No evidence for the strict no-call rule.** Shen and Tamkin's high-scoring patterns used the assistant, for explanations or conceptual questions. The evidence supports keeping generation and debugging away from the person's problem; it does not show that every assistant call harms learning. Not calling the agent at all in the reserved part is a design choice for clean measurement and enforcement.
- **No evidence on privacy, gates or overrides.** No study compares private and shared individual readings, tests a pre-merge gate, or validates the share of overridden tickets as a measure of skill drift.
- **Aviation is an analogy.** Bainbridge, Casner and the SAFOs concern process control and flight decks; their transfer to software is argued, not measured.

## 9. How the findings map to Safe Seat decisions

The boundary rule is an inference from the mechanism above. |

No study supports the confirmation step; it is a provenance control. |

Skill is tied to status, and deskilling is hard to accept ([Bainbridge §1.2](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf)). | No study tests private against shared readings. The override share as a measure of skill drift is unvalidated. The support is indirect: avoiding a new source of organizational pressure. |
| **A pre-merge gate with a recorded override by the tech lead, rather than an unexplained quota.** | The FAA leaves manual flying to the pilot in command case by case and warns against overly general rules and unexplained quotas ([SAFO 17007, pp. 2–3](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-11/SAFO17007.pdf)); automation remains appropriate under high workload ([SAFO 13002](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/other_visit/aviation_industry/airline_operators/airline_safety/SAFO13002.pdf)), which an override covers. Lack of time and seeing the work as outside one's job inhibit critical engagement ([Lee et al. §4.3.2](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)), and 30–50% of METR's developers held back tasks they did not want to do without AI ([METR 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)): a voluntary practice is likely to be skipped. | No study tests a gate's effect on skill or on burnout. The inference from METR's selection effect to developers' behavior under a gate is ours. |
